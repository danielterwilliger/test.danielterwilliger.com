A listening history

Podcasts are personal. There's an intimacy to wearing headphones and being the only one listening. It's the only medium where I catch myself doing chores in service of the content: I want to listen to this, so let me go do the dishes. It's not something you put on the TV while you scroll your phone. It can take your whole focus, and it can change the way you think.

A kid with an iPod

I grew up in a big family that moved around a lot. The year we spent in Montreal, I was a teenager and I was isolated, and that's when I dove in. I didn't have a Mac. I fantasized about having one. What I had was an iPod Classic, a Christmas gift, the one with the color screen and the click wheel, and it was my portal to everything else. I listened to MacBreak Weekly, a show about the computers I didn't own, and followed Leo Laporte's network into tech and video game podcasts. iTunes handled the shows Apple knew about. Anything else, I hunted down the raw RSS feed and added it by hand.

I understand how parasocial relationships form, because these people were basically my friends. Intimacy at a distance, for free, over an open feed. That's what this medium does, and I felt it before I could name it.

The thread I followed

The show I refreshed for was MacBreak Weekly, and the reason was Merlin Mann. I've followed Merlin's shows ever since: Back to Work on 5by5, Roderick on the Line, Reconcilable Differences on Relay FM. My father pointed me at Radiolab (I've heard every episode, some more than once) and then This American Life, which became a staple. I still keep every episode of You Look Nice Today downloaded, because some things are relistenable.

The Sunday show

The show I never miss is Le Show with Harry Shearer. It was my father's favorite. Every Sunday, sometimes falling asleep to it, waking up and rewinding. Since my father passed last year, catching Le Show every week is one of the ways I keep that connection alive.

What I believed, and how it changed

When Overcast came along I jumped from Apple's app immediately. It was a product that cared about podcast listeners. When Spotify started pulling shows onto its platform, I felt what most of the community felt: podcasts are supposed to be open and free. RSS, no gates. If a streaming service takes a show down, you lose your episodes. It's the same fight as owned files versus streaming access everywhere else in media. The economics of that era had a strange upside, too. The money mostly wasn't there, so the only shows that survived were passion projects, and that passion is how you got great shows. The sustainable version was the public radio model, the one Maximum Fun turned into a living: if you love a thing you can pay for it, but it stays free either way. Something about long-form audio wants to be treated that way.

I felt all of that a lot more strongly five years ago. I still see the downsides of closed platforms. But Serial brought the whole world into the medium, Spotify's money changed who hosts it, and "install an app and you're listening" reaches people a raw RSS feed never will. My cousin just showed me Pocket FM: serialized audio, gamified, a lot of it AI-narrated. I expected to hate it and instead I found it interesting. There's a lot of experimentation left to happen in this space. I don't know whether a raw RSS feed is still the best answer. I know the impact it had on me at the time, and I know that treating premium delivery as a settled question, in either direction, is a mistake. It's a discovery question.

I've paid for exactly one podcast

In fifteen-plus years of listening, I've supported exactly one show with money: Roderick on the Line, on Patreon. I'm the public radio listener. I'd usually rather hear the ad reads, which at this point feel like part of the show. Catching up on the Back to Work backlog right now, the old ads are archaeology: these were the services that were big in 2023.

So what converted me, once? The show had become personal long before there was a way to pay: John ran for Seattle City Council, the thing felt real, and when paying became possible it felt like supporting something real, not buying access to content. I'm your hardest listener to convert, and I converted exactly once, for relationship reasons. Understanding people like me, and the much easier listeners who aren't like me, is discovery work.

I also made one

In school, a friend and I started a podcast. Three or four episodes. We recorded a Google call, converted it through YouTube to strip the audio out, and had no good way to host it. It ended partly from scheduling, mostly from friction. I think the episodes were good, for the people who wanted them. But it's a lot of work, and we would rather have spent that time talking to each other while playing games. Making even a few episodes left me with real respect for the people who do it every week, and it showed me the creator-side tooling gap from the inside.

Podcasts taught me how to work

The early episodes of Back to Work changed how I operate. Episode 3 is literally titled "The Second Arrow": the Buddhist parable where the first arrow is the thing that happens and the second arrow is your reaction to it, and the second one is the one you can do something about. Episode 1 was about the lizard brain, the way it makes work things feel scarier than they are. There was never an alligator in the bathroom; nothing in the world changed except your perception of it. The lesson I kept: you're just a person in a room. And the mechanics I still run my work by started there: capture everything, get tasks out of your head and into a system you trust, know what done looks like. Over the years that hardened into my own rule, half GTD and half Scrum: a task doesn't exist unless it has a due date, a description of done, and an owner. A show I found through an iPod as a lonely teenager is materially why I manage products the way I do.

The listener, today

I'm on Android now. It breaks my heart that Overcast isn't there, but I understand. I tried Spotify and it didn't have everything, so it's Pocket Casts, free tier. Not thrilled, but it's fine. Life is busier with a kid, so the rotation has a hard core: Le Show is the one I always make sure I catch. Reconcilable Differences, catching up. Backlogs in progress: Roderick on the Line, This American Life, and Back to Work, which quietly ended in 2024, so the backlog is finite. Radiolab is there when I need it, and More Perfect is exactly right for the current Supreme Court moment. Listening lives where it always has: dishes, driving, chores, sometimes falling asleep.

One more observation: live streaming on Twitch and YouTube and Kick has become some of what podcasting used to be. Someone on in the background, the feeling that you're there with them. I've joined show communities (Critical Role's Discord absolutely counts). The intimacy moved. It didn't disappear. Where it goes next is an open question.
